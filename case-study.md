# Case Study Craft — Living Playbook

> This is a **living document**. Every time a new resource on storytelling / case studies /
> portfolio building is shared, its lessons get distilled and added here, and the principles
> below get updated. The goal: apply these lessons when writing and revising the case studies
> in this portfolio.

**Core idea:** *A portfolio is not a gallery — it is an argument, told through stories.*
People don't remember twelve screens. They remember the moment a problem felt real, the
choice you made, and what changed because of you.

---

## 1. The Story Flow — five beats, always in this order

1. **Character** — Who we meet, and why we should care about them.
2. **Context** — Set the stage. Where are we, and what is normal here?
3. **Conflict** — The challenge that puts something at risk.
4. **Resolution** — What *turned*. Not a perfect fix — a beat that lands.
5. **Takeaway** — The insight they can repeat tomorrow.

> Beat 4 is **resolution, not solution**. Solutions stay imperfect. "We solved it" often
> feels false. Show where things landed and what is still being pressed.

## 2. Tell it from the inside out — Why → How → What

Most case studies go outside-in (what we made → how it works → maybe why). The ones that move
people go the other way:

- **Why** — the belief, the change you wanted in someone's life. The human reason you started.
- **How** — the distinctive approach: your principles, your process, the tradeoff you'd make that others won't.
- **What** — the artifact (screens, features). Comes *last on the tongue*, even if it came first in Figma.

> "People don't buy what you do, they buy why you do it." The case study should still make
> sense if you swapped out the UI.

## 3. Begin with the customer (the "sign test")

Same fact, told from the customer's side, is a different story. Three moves that make it land:
1. **Make it about them**, not about you/the process.
2. **Put hope next to fear** — people stop when they feel what's at stake, not when they're asked.
3. **Use the word "but"** — opposition/tension is what makes it a story.

| Checklist | Weak | Strong (same thing, rewritten) |
|---|---|---|
| Start with the customer | "We redesigned the checkout." | "A customer was ready to pay, and still gave up." |
| Emotion | "Users were frustrated." | "They hoped to finish before a train, and feared looking careless at work." |
| Truth well told | "Drop-off was 42%." | "That 42% is four in ten people who wanted to pay, and didn't." |
| Contrast | "Then we improved it." | "Paying was one tap away, but the form made them feel lost. Then it didn't." |

## 4. Saying more with less — the ruthless edit

If a line doesn't change what they **feel or decide**, cut it. Run this pass out loud before sharing:

**CUT**
- Every tool name that doesn't change the decision
- Research methods listed without what they changed
- "We iterated" with no before/after
- Team-size humblebrags
- The third example that proves the same point

**KEEP**
- Who was stuck, and what it cost them
- The surprising thing you learned
- The choice you almost didn't make
- One proof: a number, a quote, a behavior change
- What you'd do differently next time

> Write the **60-second version first**: who was stuck, what you chose, what changed. If that
> isn't clear, a longer version won't save it. One story should work in a critique, a case
> study, *and* an interview.

## 5. The story checklist (run before you share)

1. **Start with the customer** — their world, their words, not your process.
2. **Connect emotionally** — hope *and* fear both on the page.
3. **Truth well told** — the fact isn't the story; how you say it is.
4. **Contrast** — before vs. after. Use the "but."
5. **Editing** — inspire, don't instruct. Kill the first draft.
6. **Storyboarding** — titles should already tell the story in a line.
7. **Cut through the clutter** — who's the stakeholder? what are they looking for?
8. **Leave a takeaway** — if they can't repeat it, it didn't land.

## 6. Titles / storyboarding

Read only the titles, in order. If they say *who, what went wrong, what turned, and what to
take away*, the deck is working. If they say "Overview, Process, Screens," it isn't.
**Don't use the default line** ("We redesigned X" could be anyone's project) — name a real moment.

## 7. Product story template — 8 lines

1. Here is the world as it is, in one scene.
2. Here is what that costs a real person.
3. Here is the belief we refused to drop.
4. Here is the choice that looked risky.
5. Here is what we made (one picture).
6. Here is how someone used it.
7. Here is what changed (number or quote).
8. Here is what is still unsolved.

> 30-second cut: keep **1, 4, 7**. If line 7 (what changed) is empty, the story isn't ready —
> the *work* might be, the *story* is not.

## 8. Telling a data story

- **Give the number a person.** Build in order: (1) human stake — who is this about? (2) before — one number, one time; (3) the turn — one sentence; (4) after — same definition as before; (5) caveat — what it doesn't prove.
- **Say one fact three ways** so nobody does the math: `33% = 3,300 people = 1 in 3 reps`.
- **Specs are features; people buy benefits.** Paint the picture first ("She hits run, then she waits"), put specs in the appendix. Use simile / analogy / metaphor.
- **Bar graphs > pie charts.** Length = comparison; you can rank without a legend. Skip 3D. One gold bar for the person you mean; the rest recede.
- If you can't say *who changed*, you have a chart, not a story.

## 9. Telling it out loud

- **True & first person** — it has to be your story to tell.
- A **specific moment**, not a five-year montage. Start where it matters; skip the warmup.
- **Stakes** (what you stood to lose) + **change** (you're different at the end).
- Sensory detail that earns its place. Memorize the first and last line, outline the rest, speak it — don't read it. If nothing was at risk, it isn't a story yet.

## 10. Presenting & handling resistance

- **The 3 D's of a meeting:** Data (you present) → Decision (they conclude) → Discussion (they talk). In 30 min: ~20 for you, 10 for them. Their discussion *is* part of the presentation.
- **First 30 seconds — pick ONE door:** Surprise · Demo · Picture · Question · Story · Quote · Destination · Short chain. Don't mix them.
- **When the room resists, diagnose before arguing:** not stupid (you jumped ahead) · missing a picture (draw it, don't repeat louder) · habit (show contrast + "why now") · no value seen yet (make the benefit obvious) · something else is on fire (name it).
- **Three inequalities:** Do > Words · Word-of-mouth > Word-of-you · Listen > Telling.

## 11. Build a bank of stories (for designers)

Capture reusable moments as they happen:

| Moment type | Example | Where it gets used |
|---|---|---|
| A user did something unexpected | Ignored the "obvious" CTA, invented their own path | Case-study insight; "tell me about a time you changed your mind" |
| You were wrong | The workshop idea everyone loved failed in testing | Opening scene of a product story |
| A tiny human detail | A nurse taped a paper workaround to the monitor | The ending your case study is missing |
| A hard conversation | You pushed back on a stakeholder and kept the relationship | Behavioral interview |
| A result you can point at | Support tickets dropped; someone said "finally" | Proof point |

## How I'll apply this to *this* portfolio

The current case studies (Vbidd, Educator AI, Wellytics, Shift Management, Nurse & Hospital,
Nurse Compliance) are structured but written **outside-in and feature-first**. To upgrade them
with these lessons:

- [ ] Open each with a **character in a moment** (a Jennifer), not "An AI-powered system that…".
- [ ] Lead **Why → How → What**; move the feature list to the end.
- [ ] Add **hope/fear/but** tension to each problem statement.
- [ ] Rewrite section titles so **titles alone tell the story**.
- [ ] Add one **proof point** per project (number, quote, or behavior change) — fill the open "Measurable impact" scores with real data.
- [ ] End each with a **takeaway + what's still unsolved**, not a tidy "we solved it."
